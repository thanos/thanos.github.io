**Galt’s Engine, Other People’s Ore**  
*Who is John Galt when the prime movers train their machines on everyone else’s mind?*

If you spend enough time building software — untangling a legacy estate, or pushing patches into a library you do not get paid to maintain — you eventually meet the industry’s private religion. For decades Silicon Valley borrowed Ayn Rand the way it borrows a whiteboard marker. The engineers and founders were the prime movers: Rearden, Dagny, Galt. The bureaucrats, regulators, and rent-seekers were the looters.

Large language models have put that fable under load. We are living through a stress test of *Atlas Shrugged* that Objectivism did not write. The people building the machines look like heroes in the lab and something closer to Orren Boyle in the hearing room. The people whose work those machines digested are being asked to treat a taking as progress.

**The Reardens of the GPU**

At first glance the fit is almost too clean. The researchers and engineers pushing neural nets are doing what Rand said producers do: they apply reason, mathematics, and brutal quantities of compute, and the result is a jump in what a single mind can finish in a day.

The political reaction fits the novel too. Proposals for licensing schemes, pre-deployment permits, and “safety” agencies with a veto over who may ship a model look, from a distance, like the State Science Institute circling Rearden Metal — a revolutionary product treated as a public hazard because it would rearrange the existing order.

That is only the first half of the story.

**The contested feedstock**

Rand’s defense of industrialists rested on a concrete claim, not a mood. You own the product of your mind when you have turned it into a work: a book, a patentable process, a piece of code released under terms you chose. She was not arguing that every idea on earth is a privately fenced pasture. She was arguing that you do not get to seize the mill and call the seizure civilization.

That is where the LLM era splits the Randian cast list.

Frontier models were not all trained on entirely licensed corpora. Their training material has included industrial crawls of text, code, and images — public-domain works, permissively licensed works, copyrighted works used without a negotiated license, and books downloaded from pirate archives. Those are different piles. The legal fight has begun to sort them.

In *Bartz v. Anthropic*, the court held that the training use at issue was fair use, while rejecting fair use for building a permanent library from pirated books. Acquisition and training were separate questions, even when they concerned the same books. Anthropic’s $1.5 billion settlement with authors is what the piracy pile looks like when it finally meets a docket. It is not a ruling that every unlicensed training use is unlawful. Nor does a finding about training turn a pirate shelf into a bookshop. [Settlement background](https://publishers.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/FAQS-Anthropic-September-5-2025-1.pdf).

From the open-source side the insult is specific, but it is not the invoice. MIT and Apache let you build a closed product and charge for it. That was part of the offer. They also came with conditions; melting the code into a model does not answer the question of whether those conditions were met. Then comes the committee. The people who helped themselves to the commons discover that the next person who wants to build a furnace should need a permit.

On a strict Randian reading, this is the part of the script nobody cast. Are the labs heroic because they built the furnace, or are they looters when the charge they poured into it was acquired without respecting the rights attached to it? The honest answer is that they can be both in the same fiscal quarter.

**The modern strike is not a valley**

In the novel the producers walk out. They take their minds to Galt’s Gulch and let the looters discover that slogans do not pour steel.

Something like that is happening. It is just not a gulch.

Authors sue. Artists reach for tools meant to frustrate training on their work. Platforms put a price on the firehose. Developers place future releases under different terms, a legally narrower and morally readable message: *this is not what I am offering you next.* That is one form a networked strike of the mind can take. It is refusal, not disappearance.

Take the analogy as far as it will go and then stop. Galt could drop off the map. We cannot, not if we still want banks, packet networks, foundries, and a cloud account. A hidden high-tech enclave that stays economically alive and legally invisible is a period piece. Exit comes with dependencies the novel could leave off the page.

Withholding can still create leverage. You can be fully visible and still refuse to offer a training license. You can be fully visible and still stop publishing the raw corpus you used to give away. You can be fully visible and still make the next decade of work harder to take without a bargain. Observation raises the price of hiding. It does not write the next library for you.

There is a colder limit. The models already ingested a large part of the public stack. A strike in 2026 is mostly a fight over the *margin*: new books, new code, new measurements, the next paper. It will not uninvent the weights that already exist. Anyone selling the fantasy of a total walkout is selling the novel, not the constraint.

**The clerical convenience and the Kafka factory**

A fair objection lands here. LLMs make red tape feel soluble. A two-person shop can turn a rulebook into a checklist, draft a model card, fill the vendor questionnaire, and sound like it has a compliance department. The old cartoon of the producer drowning in triplicate while the looter speaks fluent regulation is weaker if everyone rents the same tireless clerk.

Believe that, and stop there, and you will miss the other direction of the same tool.

Drafting costs were one brake on administrative complexity. LLMs loosen that brake. A small staff can produce another annex, another set of definitions, another evaluation protocol without making any of it cheaper to understand, challenge, or obey. Generating the forty-seventh subcategory of “AI system” becomes cheap. Deleting it is still politically expensive.

So the technology can do two jobs at once. It cheapens the paperwork for the firm trying to get through the gate. It also cheapens the paperwork for the office building it. A tireless clerk at both desks does not mean equal power on both sides.

The moat was never the PDF. The moat is permission, liability, capital, certified evaluators, compute thresholds, and the quiet fact that interpretation is a scarce resource. An LLM can write the application. It cannot grant the license. It can explain the safety case. It cannot make “state of the art” mean the same thing for a lab with a policy shop and a maintainer with a day job.

**The crony moat**

Rand’s special contempt was reserved for the businessman who invites the state in so the state will freeze his rivals. That type did not go extinct. Safety gives it a respectable vocabulary.

A proposal to gate capable models may answer a real risk. It may also make life easier for the firms already inside the gate. You do not need a conspiracy board to ask who pays the entry fee. Rules requiring evaluation infrastructure, reporting staff, and a Washington relationship will not fall evenly on a giant lab and on a student fine-tuning in a rented GPU cluster. The safety case deserves scrutiny. So does the bill.

From the trenches the temptation is familiar. First you train on a commons you did not pay to assemble. Then you discover that further competition should require a permit. Rearden built a metal. Boyle built a committee.

There is a trap here for the critics too. If every training input requires a separately negotiated license, the biggest labs can buy the warehouse while the independent developer waits outside. You can build Boyle’s committee out of contracts as well as permits. A bargain that gives creators a say should not quietly reserve the furnace for the firms that already own one.

**Who owns the increment**

The AI era does not ask you to choose between “the engineers are geniuses” and “the scrape was a taking.” Both can be true. The uncomfortable sentence is the one Silicon Valley’s Rand fans were not trained to say: a prime mover who needs a living commons of code and writing cannot treat that commons as unowned raw material and then ask the state to keep the resulting engine behind a fence.

Galt’s old question still works as a taunt. It is the wrong question now.

The right question is narrower, and it is the one a working engineer actually meets. Who controls the next increment of work — and on what terms may the engine use it?

The strike that can still matter is the refusal to donate that increment to a system that treats its creators as deposits.
