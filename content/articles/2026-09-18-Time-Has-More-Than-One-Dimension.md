Sometime around **15 BI — Before Internet** — I became fascinated by octonions. Like most people who become obsessed with a new mathematical structure, technology or idea, I immediately started looking for problems it might solve. One was whether octonions offered an interesting way of thinking about time having more than one dimension. I wasn't proposing a theory of physics; it was simply an idea that fascinated me. More than forty years later, multidimensional time is still far from mainstream physics, but it refuses to disappear. Serious researchers have explored two-time physics and other formulations involving additional temporal dimensions, and the idea periodically resurfaces in scientific discussion. It made me wonder about something more general than physics: **what happens when an idea, if true, would require us to change too much of what we already know?**

We like to think that ideas succeed or fail primarily on evidence. Eventually they should, but people don't encounter ideas without baggage. We have careers, expertise and years invested in understanding the world as it currently exists. Knowledge itself has dependencies: one result supports another, which supports another. Changing something near the bottom of that dependency graph can invalidate an extraordinary amount above it. Continental drift is a useful reminder. Wegener was not ignored because the fit of the continents was invisible; he was resisted because accepting the fit meant rebuilding geology from the ground up, and for decades there was no convincing mechanism with which to do it. Once an idea becomes embedded in a body of knowledge, removing it — or replacing it with a better one — becomes progressively more expensive. The same thing happens in a thirty-year-old software system: sometimes we leave something obviously wrong untouched because too much depends upon it.

There is also what I think of as a **hierarchy of change**. Resistance isn't necessarily greatest among the most senior people. I've often found senior scientists, engineers and executives surprisingly receptive to radical ideas. They can afford to look at the destination. The people whose daily value comes from operating the current system have a different perspective. A programmer who has spent fifteen years mastering a technology has accumulated professional capital. A new tool that makes that expertise more productive is attractive; one that makes it irrelevant is threatening. This isn't stupidity or dishonesty. Self-interest rarely announces itself as self-interest. It appears as perfectly sensible questions about maturity, support, skills, compatibility and whether something has been proven at scale.

Which leads inevitably to **follow the money**, although not quite in the usual cynical sense. Everyone may be following the money; they're simply following different money. The specialist sees the value stored in the existing system, while someone more senior may see the value released by replacing it. The same $100 million legacy platform can therefore look like an asset from one level of an organization and a $100 million opportunity from another. This helps explain why radical change sometimes produces the strange situation where the person with the original idea and the person at the top understand each other rather better than all the people between them. Their incentives happen to point toward change, even if for entirely different reasons.

Then there is **risk**, which is probably more powerful than money. An old system can be terrible and still feel safe because its failures are familiar. If I retain it and something breaks, we suffered another known problem. If I replace it with my clever new idea and something breaks, *I made the decision*. The organization's risk and the individual's risk are therefore not the same thing. Academia has its equivalent: being wrong with everybody else is relatively safe; being wrong alone can be fatal to a career, while being right alone isn't necessarily much better if it takes twenty years for everybody else to notice. Before asking whether an organization is resistant to change, it is worth asking a more precise question: **who is being asked to take the risk, and who receives the reward if it works?**

My own industry, computing, congratulates itself endlessly on innovation while demonstrating much the same behavior. We invent new frameworks and libraries constantly, yet surprisingly little has fundamentally changed in thirty years. Much of the Web is still an application parsing, generating and rendering HTML: markup designed for simple documents, stretched into an application runtime. Meanwhile we rarely reconsider the fundamental interface between human and machine. The early Web had richer competing visions — Ted Nelson's Xanadu, scientific document renderers, spatial and hypermedia interfaces. They didn't all disappear because they were technically inferior. HTML accumulated an ecosystem.

Successful ecosystems can eventually suppress alternatives. Think of an **algal bloom**: initially growth is evidence of success, but eventually the successful organism changes the environment and consumes the oxygen needed by competitors. HTML produced browsers; browsers produced sites and applications; those produced developers, tools and businesses. Eventually a new interface wasn't competing against HTML as a technology. It was competing against decades of accumulated investment around HTML. This distinction matters because we often mistake activity *within* an established paradigm for changing the paradigm. Replacing one JavaScript framework with another can be useful engineering, but it isn't necessarily innovation in the relationship between human and computer.

And now we've introduced LLMs, which add a fascinating new form of resistance. They have no career to defend and no technology investment to protect, yet their knowledge necessarily gives enormous weight to what already exists. If millions of examples describe the conventional solution and a handful describe the unconventional one, the conventional solution has statistical gravity. Ask an LLM to design an application and it naturally gravitates toward the architectures represented most heavily in its knowledge. This doesn't make LLMs hostile to innovation; they can be extraordinary tools for exploring new ideas when deliberately pushed to do so. But there is an unavoidable asymmetry worth remembering: **the past has more training data than the future.** In some sense, we have mechanized intellectual inertia.

So I return to time. It may have one dimension. Additional temporal dimensions may turn out to be useful mathematics rather than physical reality. Something much stranger may await us. That is not really my point. Imagine only that tomorrow compelling evidence appeared that our basic conception of time was wrong. The consequences would reach far beyond a single theory, which means people would experience the same evidence very differently according to what they had invested in the existing model. New ideas never compete with old ideas on an empty field; they compete with the old idea **plus everything we have already built upon it**. The question worth asking about a sufficiently disruptive idea is not only *“Is it true?”* It is also *“If it were true, who would really want to know?”*
