Reducing micro service costs: Function as a Service (FaaS) - To meet our 2020 strategic objectives, we needed to develop scalable, cloud-ready micro services, but building these services with existing infrastructure, we were faced with the following problems: Simple jobs and scripts required to be hosted on their own server. This resulted in high resource consumption where the lowest available unit is a VM, and even if we used Docker, the granularity would be too large. With every micro service, you would need to deploy an almost full stack with its own operational management. The costs and RTB complexities seemed to be exploding.FaaS was a solution, and in 2018 I wrote an in-depth POC to explore the concept. We then forked IBM's open source OpenWisk and adapted it to work on-prem and across our cloud services as RBC's own version of Amazon's Lambda service. This became a robust, scalable platform designed to support thousands of concurrent triggers and invocations and reduce the backend services explosion. Then we rewrote it from scratch and added triggering based on streamed events. This allowed massive normalization of services and economies of scale, saving the Bank millions per year in development, infrastructure, and RTB costs. mentoring experienceI operate with careful consideration, a "Five Rings" school of software development where the depth of knowledge, efficiency, simplicity, excellence, and creativity are the order of the day. 
Building a software development philosophy inspired by Miyamoto Musashi's principles in "The Book of Five Rings" can create a framework that emphasizes discipline, adaptability, and mastery. Here’s a conceptual framework:
1. The Way of Strategy (Chūdan no kurai)
Balance and Flexibility: Just as Musashi emphasizes adapting to circumstance, software development should prioritize flexibility in methodologies (e.g., Agile). Teams should be ready to pivot based on evolving requirements and circumstances.
Holistic view: Understand the entire system, not just individual components. Encourage developers to see how their work fits into the larger project goal.
2. Mastery Through Practice (Keiko)
Continuous Learning: Encourage a culture of constant learning and improvement. This harkens back to Musashi's relentless practice and study, pushing developers to refine their skills and embrace new technologies.
Code Quality: Mastery of coding practices, design patterns, and architectural principles should be a priority. Code reviews and pair programming can be tools for fostering growth.
3. Focus and Discipline (Shin)
Minimize Distractions: Just as Musashi trained himself to focus entirely on his swordsmanship, developers should cultivate an environment that minimizes distractions, allowing for deep work.
Commitment to Craft: Emphasize the importance of discipline in following best practices, including testing, documentation, and code maintenance.
4. Embrace the Void (Kū)
Innovative Thinking: Like Musashi’s concept of the void, encourage a mindset open to new ideas, exploration, and divergence from the norm. Team members should feel safe proposing unconventional solutions or innovative approaches.
Adapt to Uncertainty: Be prepared to face ambiguity and uncertainty in software requirements and technological changes, similar to the calm Musashi maintained in his confrontations.
5. The Interconnectedness of All Things (Shin'yo)
Team Collaboration: Promote strong communication and collaboration. Musashi practiced connecting with his opponents’ mindset; similarly, developers should strive to understand team dynamics and customer needs for better outcomes.
Feedback Loops: Implement regular feedback loops with stakeholders to ensure the developments align with user expectations and project objectives.
6. Physical and Mental Endurance
Balance Work and Well-being: Musashi understood the importance of physical readiness. Encourage practices that promote work-life balance and mental health, recognizing that a well-rounded developer is more effective.
Stress Management: Encourage practices such as regular breaks, mindfulness, or team-building activities to maintain morale and productivity.
7. Perseverance and Patience
Long-term Vision: Just as Musashi focused on long-term mastery rather than quick wins, software projects should prioritize sustainable development and avoid shortcuts that might compromise quality.
Resilience Against Challenges: Build a culture that views obstacles as opportunities for learning and growth, reflecting Musashi’s overcoming of physical and mental challenges.
Conclusion
By adopting these principles derived from Musashi's philosophy, software development can become a disciplined, adaptable, and collaborative practice that emphasizes mastery and a deep understanding of the art, both in crafting code and in working as a team. This philosophy fosters continuous improvement and resilience, essential traits in the ever-evolving tech landscape.

bio
I started in a roundabout way with Computer Science. At eighteen, I had no idea what a computer was. I had never seen one and had no curiosity in this direction. I was good at maths, but my heart was set on being a hydrologist and working with developing countries solving their water problems. I was an electronics and HiFi enthusiast, but on a more prosaic side, and had a side business trading and repairing valve amplifiers and other vintage equipment. I got a summer job with the Electric Coin company, one of the most prominent vendors of slot machines, but to my dismay, instead of wielding a soldering iron found myself coding 6801 controllers. By the end of the summer, it's sad to say, I was addicted to programming!
So at the last minute, I jumped ship from a pretty good college and went to the North London Polytechnic and took Maths & Computer Science. While at college, I worked for Richard Forsyth, coding his Darwin genetic programming framework – one of the first Machine Learning platforms - which we used as an oracle to lose our money on the horses. I graduated in 1985 and joined Atari's PC games division. I then left Atari to create my own startup specializing in RIP engines for printer manufactures. I kind of remained a freelancer until this current position with RBC CM. 
If my grandchildren were ever to ask me, "What did you do in your life..." I would be hard placed to mention anything remarkable. Much of my life has been programming in itself, which has not left a visible trail. 
At the beginning of my career in the UK, I avoided financial institutions except for a gig with Barclays, for whom I developed their Topic-based trading system. But my work included coding the standard libraries for Walter Bright's C++ distribution; porting Eispack and Linpack to C++ and ADA; writing a Forth based RIP engines for Panasonic's laser printers; writing most of the drivers for their fax machines; implementing the functional programming language, Hope, for Imperial College and British Telecom; developing a fast time-series database for telecom for BT; another database for Multitone – a major beeper manufacturer; video routing switches for the BBC; OR (pallet packing) and then fuzzy logic control systems for Unilever; and an Occam based neural network on Transputers for the UK Treasury. During this period, I also worked for Ferranti to design and develop a real-time operating system for their Lynx helicopter program. The early nineties started with an interest in PDAs, and I joined Apple's Newton program developing programming languages and programming tools for the growing Newton community. It was through working for Apple in 1993 that I discovered the internet. That year, I started developing a centralized internet-based (using WAIS!) patient register and records for the UK's National Health system. In 1994 I joined DG1 in Luxembourg as the IT advisor to the European Parliament president, Klaus Hänsch. While there, I got into workflow systems and developed their all-embracing internet-based workflow system that is still in use today. In 1995 I got a commission to build an internet casino for Caesars. The casino presented many new security and session management challenges in online development and design. It was this project that made me a Python evangelist. I had first developed the Casino in C++, but I just could complete it. The codebase was tight but still over 40k lines. On a bored Friday night in Luxembourg, I bought the Mark Lutz Python book. I rewrote my entire code in Python over the weekend and looked at something much better sitting at 4k LOC! For the casino to take online payments, I worked with the Marita bank and developed an early cyber currency, WebCheck. After Luxembourg, I moved to Dusseldorf to work at Westdeutsche Landesbank to develop its VAR system. The finance world was alien to me, and after a year, I bailed out and joined Ericsson on their distributed switch programming system based on Erlang. From Stockholm, I moved to Zurich. I got pouched by IBM's research labs near to code their mobile phone version of the JVM.
In 1997 I returned to my hometown, New York, and started developing a new NYSE trading system. It was NYSE's first internet-based system and possibly the first financial production system written in Python. Since then, I've been mostly working on finance gigs. I've developed high- frequency trading systems, trading platforms for fixed income, and risk systems for commodities at various joints such as ICAP, BrokerTec, a couple of times for JP Morgan, Credit Suisse, and this also my second time with RBC CM. There have been some sojourns with a few startups, including mobile apps, social media analytics, blockchains, smart contacts, and alt-currencies.
